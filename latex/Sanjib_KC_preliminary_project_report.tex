%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
Aircrew and passengers are exposed to cosmic radiation, but the dose rate is
measured on only a few flights, so operational estimates rely on physics
models that underpredict the largest doses. We train deep networks to correct
a physics-based dose model using in-flight measurements and the preceding hour
of space-weather and atmospheric conditions. On a held-out test set of 29,294
observations, a current-time multilayer perceptron reduces overall error by
5.3\% relative to the physics model, while a residual Transformer trained with
a high-dose-weighted loss reduces error above 15~$\mu$Sv/h by 13\%. These
results show that learned correction of a physics model is effective, and
that the choice of training objective matters most for the rare high-dose
cases that are most relevant to radiation safety.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles produce secondary radiation
in the atmosphere, and its intensity at cruising altitude is many times higher
than at the ground. Aircrew are therefore among the most radiation-exposed
occupational groups, and accurate estimates of the effective dose rate are
needed for exposure records and for flight decisions during solar events.
Direct measurements, such as those from the Automated Radiation Measurements
for Aerospace Safety (ARMAS) system~\cite{tobiska2016}, are available for only
a small number of flights, so routine estimates depend on models.

The current operational approach uses physics-based transport models such as
NAIRAS~\cite{mertens2013} and CARI-7~\cite{copeland2017}. These models are
global and physically interpretable, but they simplify the near-Earth
environment, and NAIRAS systematically underpredicts the largest ARMAS
measurements. Our earlier static machine-learning study showed that geospace
properties can reduce this error~\cite{kc2025}, but it used only the conditions
at the observation time and ignored how the environment evolved beforehand.
Standard training losses also favor the abundant low-dose cases over the rare
high-dose cases that matter most for safety.

We address these gaps with three choices. First, rather than predicting dose
rate from scratch, the network learns a correction to NAIRAS V3, so that the
physics model supplies the baseline and the network supplies what it misses.
Second, a Transformer encoder~\cite{vaswani2017} reads the preceding 55 minutes
of 35 environmental variables to test whether recent history adds information.
Third, a weighted Huber loss up-weights observations above 15~$\mu$Sv/h. Our
contributions are: (i) a leakage-controlled comparison of current-time,
convolutional, and attention models on 92,415 flight-separated observations;
(ii) evidence that high-dose weighting changes which model should be selected;
and (iii) an occlusion analysis of which variables and time lags the
correction depends on.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Physics-based aviation dose models.}
NAIRAS~\cite{mertens2013} and CARI-7~\cite{copeland2017} propagate cosmic-ray
spectra through the geomagnetic field and atmosphere to compute dose at any
location and altitude. They require no flight measurements, which is their
main strength, but their simplified inputs cause systematic biases that
measurement campaigns such as ARMAS~\cite{tobiska2016} have exposed. We do not
replace these models; we keep NAIRAS V3 as the baseline and learn its error.

\textbf{Machine learning for space weather.}
Camporeale~\cite{camporeale2019} reviews machine-learning nowcasting and
forecasting in space weather and highlights limited data, rare extreme events,
and the need for physically meaningful validation. Sadykov
\etal~\cite{sadykov2026} released machine-learning-ready datasets that align
ARMAS and NAIRAS with aircraft position, neutron-monitor counts, solar-wind
parameters, particle fluxes, and geomagnetic indices, and our earlier
work~\cite{kc2025} used the static version to correct NAIRAS. That work treats
each observation independently; here we model the preceding hour explicitly
and train on flight-separated partitions of the time-series dataset.

\textbf{Physics-guided learning.}
Theory-guided data science~\cite{karpatne2017} argues that combining physical
models with data-driven learning improves generalization and consistency
compared with either approach alone. Learning the residual of a physics model
is one simple form of this idea. We apply it to aviation dose and test it
against direct prediction with otherwise identical architectures.

\textbf{Temporal attention and imbalanced targets.}
Transformers~\cite{vaswani2017} and the Temporal Fusion
Transformer~\cite{lim2021} model multivariate time series through
self-attention and can provide interpretable temporal weighting. Unlike those
long-horizon forecasting settings, our sequence is short and highly
redundant, so whether attention helps is an open question we test directly.
For skewed targets, Yang \etal~\cite{yang2021} show that standard regression
losses underperform in sparse target regions. We use a simpler remedy, a
threshold-weighted robust Huber loss~\cite{huber1964}, because the
high-dose threshold is defined operationally.

\section{Model and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data and Leakage Control}
Each record contains a $12\times35$ temporal tensor of environmental
variables sampled every five minutes and eight static flight and geomagnetic
variables. Source arrays store the current value at index zero, so each array
is reversed once into chronological order from $t-55$ to $t$ minutes. The
vehicle identifier is excluded to prevent the model from memorizing aircraft.
The dataset is split by flight into three partitions
(Table~\ref{tab:partitions}): P1 is used for training and for fitting median
imputation and standardization, P2 for early stopping and model selection,
and P3 only for the final test. We denote this assignment 123. As
Table~\ref{tab:partitions} shows, about 16\% of each partition lies above
15~$\mu$Sv/h, and Fig.~\ref{fig:target} shows the resulting right-skewed
target distribution.

\subsection{Physics-Guided Residual Formulation}
Let $A_i$ be the ARMAS measurement and $N_i$ the NAIRAS V3 estimate for
observation $i$, with temporal inputs $X_i$ and static inputs $s_i$. The
residual models learn the physics-model error and reconstruct the dose rate as
\begin{equation}
r_i = A_i - N_i, \qquad
\hat{A}_i = N_i + f_\theta(X_i, s_i, N_i),
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta$ is a neural network with parameters $\theta$ trained to
predict $r_i$. Direct models instead predict $A_i$ without $N_i$.

\subsection{Network Architectures}
Table~\ref{tab:models} lists the four architectures. The joint multilayer
perceptron (JMLP) concatenates the eight static and 35 current environmental
inputs and applies 128- and 64-unit GELU layers with normalization and
dropout. The hybrid CNN--MLP applies two one-dimensional convolutions over the
12-step history and joins the pooled representation with a current-input
branch. The Transformer projects each 35-variable time step to 48 dimensions,
adds sinusoidal positional encoding and a learned summary token, and applies
two four-head pre-normalized encoder blocks~\cite{vaswani2017}; a separate MLP
embeds the current inputs. The residual Transformer is the same network
trained on Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual}.

\subsection{Training Objective}
All networks minimize a weighted Huber loss~\cite{huber1964},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,
\ell_\delta\!\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right), \qquad
w_i = \begin{cases} w, & A_i > 15,\\ 1, & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\label{eq:huber}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_\delta$ is the Huber function, $y_i$ is the standardized target,
and $w\in\{1,2,4\}$ for the residual Transformer and $w=1$ for all other
models. Training uses AdamW, cosine learning-rate decay, gradient clipping at
1.0, batch size 512, at most 24 epochs, and early stopping with patience five.
Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows the validation losses used for checkpoint
selection.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
We report root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error, coefficient of
determination $R^2$, and bias, both overall and for $A_i\leq15$ and $A_i>15$
$\mu$Sv/h. NAIRAS V3 is evaluated on exactly the same P3 observations as every
network. To interpret the residual Transformer, group occlusion replaces one
standardized temporal variable, or one complete time lag, with its P1 mean and
records the increase in P2 RMSE; this measures model dependence, not physical
causation. Code, checkpoints, per-epoch histories, and a run manifest with
random seed 42 are available at
\url{https://github.com/thesanjibkc/Deep_learning_project}.

\subsection{Test-Set Accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rmse} compare all models on P3.
The JMLP, selected on P2, reaches RMSE 3.778~$\mu$Sv/h, MAE 2.793, and
$R^2=0.506$, a 5.3\% reduction from NAIRAS V3. The residual Transformer with
$w=2$ has the lowest P3 RMSE (3.712), but we do not use the test ranking to
revise the selection. The CNN--MLP is worse than NAIRAS, showing that adding
temporal capacity does not guarantee improvement.

The high-dose results support the weighted objective. Increasing $w$ from 1 to
4 lowers high-dose RMSE from 6.602 to 5.650~$\mu$Sv/h and high-dose bias from
$-5.064$ to $-3.513$~$\mu$Sv/h, while overall RMSE rises only from 3.811 to
3.822. Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} shows the predictions of the selected JMLP;
its high-dose RMSE (6.571) is 1.7 times its overall RMSE, so the remaining
error is concentrated in the largest measurements.

\subsection{Temporal Interpretation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:occlusion} shows that occluding the Newark (0.030), Oulu
(0.025), and Thule (0.011) neutron monitors and the solar F10.7 flux (0.009)
causes the largest RMSE increases. No single-lag occlusion increases RMSE,
and the current time step has the least negative change. The history is
therefore highly redundant, which helps explain why attention does not
outperform the current-time JMLP overall.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Learning a correction to a physics model is more effective than learning dose
rate from scratch: with identical architectures, the residual Transformer
beats the direct Transformer, and neutron-monitor counts carry most of the
correction. Temporal complexity, however, is not automatically useful. The
preceding hour is highly redundant, and a 61,000-parameter attention model
trained on 32,369 observations does not beat a compact current-time network
overall. Finally, the evaluation objective changes the conclusion: when
high-dose underprediction is the concern, the weighted residual Transformer is
the best choice. These are single-seed, single-split results, and observations
within a flight are correlated. The final study will therefore repeat the
models over all six train--validation--test assignments and several seeds,
and add integrated-gradient and attention diagnostics.
